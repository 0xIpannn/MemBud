# CHAPTER 5 DISCUSSION

This chapter interprets the pinned-revision results of Chapter 4. It states the negative result precisely, explains why the plain-text comparison was misleading, and connects both to the graph-memory hypothesis and to evaluation practice. The numbers are Chapter 4's counts and none is recomputed here; no result goes beyond the 36-case, single-reviewer, synthetic instrument. Results are in the past tense and interpretation in the present. Labels remain single-reviewer exploratory annotations with a second independent review pending, and a zero difference is read as "not detectable at this sample size", never as equivalence.

## 5.1 From Measurement to Interpretation

RQ1 asked whether guarded verification reduced transition errors relative to an unguarded pipeline, and whether a graph verifier detected more errors than a non-graph verifier applying the same criteria. Chapter 4 answered both on the frozen instrument: the guarded pipeline behaved as designed, while the graph-versus-text half showed no detectable decision-level advantage once information access was equalized.

The guarded conditions behaved as the design intends. Both accepted all 14 labelled-valid updates in the rules-only run with zero false holds on that denominator, and both classified all 10 expected-invalid transitions as `INVALID_INPUT` before verification without writing memory. Graph held all 12 expected-`HOLD` cases, while plain text held 10 of 12 and committed the two annotated-contradiction cases. The unguarded reference applied all 26 schema-valid candidates, including all 12 expected-`HOLD` candidates (Tables 4.3, 4.4, 4.9). These were exact counts on 36 single-reviewer synthetic cases, not population estimates or causal effects.

The negative result concerns the second half. On the 36 paired rules-only cases the guarded conditions differed on exactly two, `DEV-13` and `HELD-13`, both annotated unresolved contradictions, where graph returned `HOLD` and plain text `ACCEPT` (Table 4.8). A fair text control with the same contradiction access changed exactly those two decisions and then agreed with graph on all 36 cases, with zero paired differences (Table 4.10) — hence also on all 26 non-invalid cases. No decision-level representation advantage was detectable at n = 36 after information access was equalized.

That claim is bounded on both sides. It is not equivalence: a zero paired difference at this sample size shows that the design did not detect a difference, not that none exists, and equivalence would require sufficient power, independent review, and positive evidence that no difference exists where one could arise. It is also not graph inferiority: the audit compared decisions and did not measure the structural checks that defend the graph verifier against malformed input or let it attribute a failure, capabilities this instrument did not exercise.

The design separates two effects, and the evidence supports one. The contrast from plain text to the fair text control isolates the value of contradiction information access; the contrast from the fair text control to graph holds that information equal and isolates verifier construction. The first moved exactly two decisions; the second moved none. Annotation access therefore explains the visible difference, and what looked like a property of the representation was a property of which fields the verifier could see.

## 5.2 Why the Plain-Text Comparison Was Misleading

The plain text baseline is the historical control: exact claim-set checks over the canonical transition, evaluating coverage, preservation and faithfulness, implemented independently of the graph verifier, and deliberately not reading the `evidence.contradicts` field (Section 3.2.5). The graph verifier does read it, and on identical frozen transitions that asymmetry produced the only two disagreements, `DEV-13` and `HELD-13`, both `contradiction_unresolved`, both expected `HOLD`, both accepted by text and held by graph.

Two differences in thirty-six invite the reading that the representation made the difference. The audit shows why that reading is wrong: a fair text control given the same claim-set checks plus the same contradiction access changed exactly those two decisions and agreed with graph elsewhere. The remaining failure-reason counts were identical across the guarded conditions — 4 `PRIOR_RECORD_UNAUTHORIZED_CHANGE`, 8 `REQUIRED_CLAIM_OMITTED`, 8 `UNSUPPORTED_CLAIM` — while `CONTRADICTED_CLAIM` was 2 for graph and 0 for text, with both observing 78 checks (Table 4.11). The verifiers examined the same facts and failed on the same cases except where one was not reading the relevant field.

The methodological lesson outlasts the negative result. A representation comparison is interpretable only when the compared verifiers receive equal information; otherwise it measures field access, not representation. Reporting what each verifier can see, constructing an information-matched control, and pre-specifying contrasts produces a result that could be attributed to structure if a difference survived. That is a transferable contribution: an information-parity audit is cheap, deterministic and reusable, and it changes what a comparison is allowed to mean. It does not establish how common such confounds are in the published literature; it shows that here the confound was decisive and the audit removed it.

The audit must not be over-read either. The fair control is a minimal construct built for the audit — claim-set checks plus contradiction access — not a production verifier, so its agreement with graph is evidence about these two implementations on this instrument, not proof that any text verifier matches any graph verifier. The audit also exercises only contradiction annotations; it does not probe multi-hop provenance or derivation, contradictions across records, or temporal ordering and supersession, which are the mechanisms through which graph structure might be expected to contribute. Those mechanisms were not annotated as decision-relevant, so the audit could not have detected an advantage from them.

## 5.3 The Cost of Conservatism

Fail-closed verification buys safety at the price of false holds, and the two runs priced that trade differently. In the rules-only run the cost was not observable: both guarded conditions accepted 14 of 14 labelled-valid updates with zero false holds, graph held all 12 expected-`HOLD` cases and text held 10 of 12 (Table 4.4), so conservatism under deterministic rules gave up no measured valid acceptance. The held-out model-based run told a different story: graph-LLM held 4 of 7 labelled-valid updates, for valid acceptance 3/7, whereas text-LLM held 1 of 7 and accepted 6 of 7, with 0/6 false accepts and 6/6 expected-`HOLD` detection in both (Table 4.12). The semantic condition accepted all 7 labelled-valid updates with zero false holds but recorded one false accept, `HELD-13`, because at the evaluated revision `4190526` its semantic module did not consume `evidence.contradicts`. On this split the trade-off was real and moved in opposite directions across conditions.

Interpretation has to stop where the evidence stops. The artifacts record the decisions and failed checks for the graph false holds but not their cause, so this chapter does not attribute the false holds to serialisation length, token budget or any other mechanism. Nor can the instrument price the trade-off it records. Downstream question answering was not measured, so the utility lost when a valid update is held and the harm avoided when a faulty one is rejected are both unpriced; acceptance is a safety decision about state faithfulness and prior-memory preservation and says nothing about whether a later answer would be correct. The held-out split yields 13 executed rows per condition, 7 labelled-valid and 6 expected-`HOLD`, the labels are single-reviewer exploratory annotations, and the LLM conditions differed in budget: graph used 63,211 tokens and a mean latency of 20,432.41 ms against 28,487 tokens and 8,533.35 ms for text, ratios of approximately 2.22 and 2.39 as descriptive arithmetic (Table 4.13). Those ratios are not tested differences and not an equal-budget comparison; billed cost was unavailable because the provider reported none, so only a mechanical estimate range exists (Table 4.16). The trade-off is real but unpriced: a limitation, not a finding about which operating point is preferable.

Safety properties should be kept separate from the accuracy comparison, because they do not depend on which condition "wins". The guarded path rejects schema-invalid transitions before verification and never writes for them; `HOLD`, invalid input, stale prior state and a failed apply leave host state untouched or restore it where possible; the reference tests find in-memory records and the SQLite file's SHA-256 identical before and after a hold, and every outcome appends a decision record (Table 4.17). These are properties of the policy, and they frame the cost honestly: a conservative policy has the defined failure mode of a delayed valid update rather than the undefined one of a persisted error.

## 5.4 Modularity and the Pre-Commit Boundary

RQ2 asked whether the same verification logic can operate across host backends through adapters, and what information each adapter requires and loses. The evaluated revision answered partially. A structural adapter protocol defines `backend_name`, a `snapshot(scope)` that converts host state into canonical records, and an atomic `replace_state(scope, records)` that replaces the whole scoped state. The reference backend implements it twice, in memory and over SQLite, and `GuardedMemory` fixes the interception order: capture the prior state, build the canonical transition with invalid input rejected before any write, verify through the audited path, persist only on `ACCEPT` after re-snapshotting and comparing against the captured prior state, and append one decision record per proposal. The repository tests exercise `ACCEPT`-only application, byte-identical holds, pre-write rejection of invalid changes, and decision-record ordering (Table 4.17). On two reference storage modes the verification layer can be interposed and can fail safely.

The more consequential result is structural and negative: the inspected third-party systems expose no safe public pre-commit boundary, so guarding them would require host-owned staging or a fork. Mem0 at `mem0ai==2.2.1` offers no public callback, transaction or pre-commit hook exposing the complete proposed transition; addition computes a candidate set internally and then writes several stores sequentially, and update and delete mutate the vector store before writing history and entity-store links. A-Mem at commit `ceffb860f0712bbae97b184d440df62bc910ca8d` calls `process_memory()` on note addition, which can mutate neighbouring notes before the new note is persisted, and public update writes the retriever by delete and re-add (Table 4.18). Neither was installed or exercised, and no compatibility is claimed. The boundary the layer needs — a complete candidate transition available before any persistence — does not exist as a public surface in these releases, which is a deployment obstacle, not an integration result.

Modularity also has a non-code cost. An adapter must map host state to canonical records while preserving record identity and claim boundaries, expose scopes as plain mappings, and document the provenance, timestamps, relationships, metadata and host-side effects the mapping loses; evidence and proposed operations travel in the transition and adapters must not invent them (Table 4.17). The layer is generic over hosts that offer a replaceable scope; the mapping is where information goes missing. The reference integration also records limits: the JSON baseline and host backend are separate writes with no distributed transaction, and only adapters whose replacement is atomic or restorable can be labelled guarded.

The RQ2 scope should be stated plainly. This revision demonstrates a contract, a reference backend and a feasibility assessment; the two reference backends implement the same contract rather than being independent third-party systems, and the three-backend evaluation remains future work. The study does not show that MemBud runs across three backends, that any third-party host is compatible, that no information is lost in a real mapping, or that the layer is production-ready, and none of those claims is made. What it does establish is that verification-before-commit is implementable and fail-safe when the host supplies a pre-commit boundary, and that for hosts that do not, staging the candidate outside the write path is the practical path, which makes integration architectural before it is an interface exercise.

## 5.5 What Would Count as Evidence for a Graph Advantage?

The negative result does not say a graph advantage is impossible. It says this instrument produced no decision-level evidence of one after equalizing information access, and implies what a future test would need to look like.

First, the case material would need decision-relevant relational structure that a fair text verifier cannot reconstruct from the same canonical facts: multi-hop provenance or derivation paths, contradictions holding between relations across different records rather than inside one annotated field, temporal ordering and supersession, or entity-relation constraints checkable only when relations are explicit. The cases would have to be annotated so that the correct decision depends on that structure; otherwise adding graph edges over the same facts cannot change a decision, as the audit already showed.

Second, the comparison would need information-matched controls: a fair text baseline extended with the same relations, or a canonical-facts baseline receiving every derivable relation as an explicit claim. A surviving difference could then be attributed to representation, with each condition's field access reported alongside its decisions, as the audit does.

Third, the held-out set would need to be powered and carry independent annotation: a second reviewer with an agreement statistic, a larger pre-specified case set, and resampling intervals declared in advance as the planned analysis. The present study computed no power, significance or interval.

Fourth, contrasts would need to be pre-specified and stratified by error type — contradiction, provenance, temporal ordering — rather than collapsed into a composite score, for the same reason the present study reports paired differences with case identifiers: a composite would hide which cases drove an effect.

Fifth, a cost model would need to price the trade-off. An advantage would have to be worth its false-hold cost and its token and latency overhead, which requires downstream question answering under a documented licence review, equal-budget conditions and a billed rather than estimated cost. The present study measured tokens, attempts and latency and could measure neither billed cost nor downstream utility.

Several results would not count. A better prompt is a prompt difference, not a representation difference. A longer serialisation that includes more of the same facts is a length or information difference, and the graph-to-text ratios in Table 4.13 are a warning rather than an advantage. A comparison against a verifier that ignores a field is a comparison of information access. A single differing case, or one that dissolves under an information-matched control, is not structural inference. A claim that one representation is inherently better than the other, or that this study refutes graph memory, is licensed by neither outcome. The next experiment implied by this study is therefore to add relation-dependent cases, extend the fair text control with the same relations, give the semantic check access to contradiction annotations, run under an equal token budget with pre-specified stratified contrasts, and add a licence-reviewed downstream question-answering condition. That programme tests the narrowed hypothesis of Section 5.7, not the broad hypothesis that graph memory improves agent memory as such.

## 5.6 Threats to Validity

Section 3.2.6 framed the threats and this discussion does not restate that framework; it records only what Chapter 4 made concrete. Construct validity is the central issue: the labels operationalise safety as a commit gate, which is defensible for the stated research question but is not downstream answer quality, and the structural checks that distinguish a graph verifier are not measured as decision-level detection power. The cases were authored alongside the verifiers, so circularity cannot be ruled out. The labels are single-reviewer exploratory annotations, no second annotation has been collected, no agreement statistic exists, and the second review is pending.

Internal validity rests on identical frozen inputs, candidate and decision policy, with model, prompt versions, temperature, output schema and retry policy pinned for the model-based conditions. The residual confound is that graph and text serialisations differ in length and therefore in token and latency budget, which was instrumented and reported but cannot be removed post hoc. Each model-based condition was executed once over 13 rows; temperature 0 and JSON mode reduce but do not certify run-to-run invariance, and no cache hits were recorded. The fairness audit is deterministic, so its zero difference is not a sampling artifact, but it is an artifact of the control as constructed. The causes of the graph false holds were not established by the pinned artifacts.

Statistical conclusion validity is limited by 36 cases, 18 per split, and a single reviewer. No significance test, power analysis, confidence interval or multiplicity adjustment was computed; zero differences are reported as not detectable at this size, never as equivalence; and bootstrap intervals are a planned analysis for a larger set with independent annotation rather than a result at n = 36.

External validity is limited by construction: the instrument is synthetic Indonesian-themed fiction, contains no production traffic, exercises a reference backend rather than a third-party integration, and measures no downstream task. Measurement limits compound this: billed monetary cost was unavailable and only an estimated range exists, token counts are telemetry rather than a billing record, rules-run timings are sub-millisecond and timer-noise dominated, and no question-answering utility was measured.

One reproducibility decision should be stated separately because it could be mistaken for a result. The paper is pinned to the committed artifact set at `1ce84ce` for the rules-only run and the fairness audit, and to the code revision its artifacts record, `4190526`, for the held-out model-based run. The rules run records a working-tree digest rather than a git commit, so it is not claimed to be pinned to `1ce84ce`. The evaluated revision predates later repository work; keeping the pinned basis keeps every claim traceable to a committed artifact, while re-pinning would require regenerating the evidence chain and is not done here. The semantic condition's behaviour on `HELD-13` is an integration gap of revision `4190526`, not a statement about semantic-model quality. The study involved no human subjects and no user data and used no licensed data.

## 5.7 Implications

For the graph-memory hypothesis the implication is neither confirmation nor refutation but narrowing. The v2 evidence does not support a representation-level advantage at the decision level after information access is equalized, and the fairness audit shows why earlier apparent advantages must be re-tested under information parity before they are attributed to structure. The hypothesis that survives is specific: graph memory should matter when relations among records, entities, evidence and time carry decision-relevant information that cannot be reconstructed from the same canonical facts. That hypothesis is testable in the way Section 5.5 describes. The evidence does not show that graph memory is useless: graph structures remain well motivated for organising and retrieving memory, as in linked-note and temporal-knowledge-graph designs [@xu2025amem; @rasmussen2025zep], and structural checks matter for defending against malformed graph input and attributing failures — but those are different claims, and this study did not measure them.

For evaluation practice the contribution is a reusable information-parity audit: report what each verifier can read, construct a control that reads the same fields through a different mechanism, pre-specify paired contrasts so each isolates one effect, and report counts with explicit denominators and case identifiers rather than a composite score. A representation comparison without the audit is liable to measure annotation access and report the result as structure. Small-sample discipline belongs to the same contribution: at 36 cases a zero difference is not equivalence, a single differing case is not a representation effect, and denominators and labels must be stated every time a count is used.

For builders, verification-before-commit is implementable and can be fail-safe on a reference backend: invalid input is rejected before any write, `HOLD` leaves the backend byte-identical, and every outcome appends a decision record. The obstacle to adopting the layer is the absence of a safe pre-commit boundary in the inspected releases, which makes host-owned staging or a fork the realistic path rather than a drop-in adapter [@chhikara2025mem0; @xu2025amem]. The adapter contract also prices integration: record identity, claim boundaries, provenance, timestamps and relations must come from the host and be documented where they are lost, making integration architectural before it is an interface problem.

The overall contribution is best stated in three parts, which Chapter 6 develops. It provides an artefact — a guarded memory-verification pipeline with an adapter contract and a reference backend exercised on two storage modes — together with evidence that the layer can fail closed. It provides a reusable information-parity protocol for comparing memory representations without confounding information access with mechanism. And it provides a rigorous, bounded negative result: on a 36-case, single-reviewer, synthetic instrument, no decision-level representation advantage was detectable after information access was equalized, which is neither equivalence nor inferiority and is not a population or production claim. What remains is an independent second review of the labels, a larger powered held-out set, a contradiction-aware semantic verifier and a relation-extended fair text baseline, a staged integration with a real backend, and downstream question answering under a licence-reviewed dataset. Chapter 6 concludes by research question and sets out that future work.
